The evaluation starts with the confirmatory test, which compares the four pipelines under the rules fixed before the test. It then examines retrieval components, traces where wrong answers come from and measures what a selection step adds. Next, the evidence budget is varied, and iterative search, long-context reading and routing are compared with single-pass pipelines. The fourth part compares Persian and English, and the last part checks how far the rankings survive a repeated run of the reader, a second judge and a different review design, and turns the findings into guidance. Unless noted, scores are for the primary reader and the first judge on the 90 answerable questions. A net value is the number of answers by which a variant exceeds its reference, and a pair such as 11/4 gives the number of questions that only the first variant answers correctly and the number that only the second does. Ranges such as 62--63 cover the answers that the judge could not score (Sect.~\ref{sec:readers}).

The accuracy gate was not met. At the tight budget \lexb{} answered 5 to 7 more of the 90 answerable questions than \hybrid{}, but between 0 and 3 fewer than \hr{}, so it missed the required margin of 5 over \hr{}. Neither primary test was significant. % src: R1: paired.tex (pre-cut), revision_stats.md; net +5..+7 (11/4), sign p .118--.332, component CI [-4.3, 17.7]; LEX5B-HR -3..0 (6/7), p .607--1, CI [-10.3, 7.5]
\lexb{} minus \hybrid{} is +5 to +7 answers (11/4; sign $p$ from .12 to .33; 95\% interval from $-4.3$ to $+17.7$ percentage points), and \lexb{} minus \hr{} is $-3$ to 0 (6/7; $p$ from .61 to 1; interval from $-10.3$ to $+7.5$). The intervals resample groups of questions that share a source, and both include zero. % src: R2: TEST_RESULTS.md sec. 2 tokens/q (HYBRID 2216, HR 2236, LEX5B 4511-5130, SETR 7502-8127), revision_stats.md tokens (2.04-2.32x); limits 1.5 and 2.5 = PROTOCOL.md sec. 5
At 6,000 characters \hybrid{} and \hr{} use approximately 2,200 query-time tokens per question, \lexb{} 4,500 to 5,100 and \setr{} 7,500 to 8,100. \lexb{} thus used 2.0 to 2.3 times the tokens of \hybrid{}, which is within the relaxed limit of 2.5 and above the original limit of 1.5, so the resource gate passed only under the relaxed limit. Single facts (T1) are near ceiling for every pipeline, so a selection step has little room to help there. On within-document questions (T2), \lexb{} and \hr{} gain over \hybrid{} while \setr{} does not, and on cross-document questions (T3), \hr{} and \setr{} lead. The development results also shrank on the test, as expected for a method that was adapted to the development set over many iterations: the lead of \lexb{} over \hybrid{} roughly halved, and its lead over \hr{} disappeared (from +11 and +6 percentage points on development to +6 and 0 on the test, under the merged scores). % src: R11: devtest.tex (pre-cut) and jA_appendix l.174: dev +11.1 and +5.6 after two corrections; test merged +5.6 and 0.0 (TEST_RESULTS_HUMAN_SYM.md)
The confirmatory result is therefore a null result with a wide interval, and it says little about which setup to prefer.

Three post hoc analyses qualify this result. First, in the merged scores, in which the verdicts of the author replace the judge's on the 30 reviewed questions, the four pipelines answer between 71 and 78 of the 90 questions. The three pipelines with a reranking or selection step have higher point estimates than \hybrid{}, \lexb{} ties \hr{}, and the difference between \lexb{} and \hybrid{} is not significant after Holm correction over the two primary comparisons (corrected $p=.36$). \setr{} has the highest merged score, but its lead over \hybrid{} (7 wins and no loss, $p=.016$) is one of six uncorrected comparisons. Second, an equivalence test \citep{schuirmann1987tost} on the first-judge scores fails to show that \lexb{} and \hr{} are equivalent even for a margin of 8 questions out of 90. Third, a power calculation based on Eq.~\ref{eq:mde} shows that a paired comparison has 80\% power only for differences of approximately 11 answers. A difference of 5 answers, which was the gate, would be detected in fewer than one case in five and would need approximately 400 to 460 questions. The gate was therefore likely to fail whether or not selection helps, and its failure is weak evidence in either direction.

The channels differ in what they find before any reader is involved. Among the top 40 candidates, BM25 and dense retrieval find a complete evidence bundle for almost the same number of Persian questions, and over both languages the dense list holds one for 71 of the 90 questions and the BM25 list for 66. BM25 depends on text normalisation: 27 of the 45 Persian questions contain Persian digits, which match the ASCII digits of the documents only after normalisation. Recall is not accuracy, however. \hybrid{} beats both single channels at the tight budget, by 9 to 10 answers over dense retrieval and by even more over BM25, as reported for hybrid retrieval elsewhere \citep{bruch2023fusion}. The gap to BM25 survives Bonferroni correction over the 12 declared comparisons, whereas the gap to dense retrieval does not. Fusion helps through ordering rather than recall, since the dense list alone holds a complete bundle almost as often as the hybrid list, and a larger budget removes most of the gap.

Three further changes did not improve on \hybrid{}. HyDE \citep{gao2023hyde} and multi-query rewriting both scored below the unrewritten query, by 1 to 5 answers at one extra language-model call per question, and they lowered the number of complete bundles that reached the reader, mostly in Persian. Chunks of 1,000 characters gave the highest accuracy. Chunks of 2,000 characters lowered it by 13 to 20 answers, a loss that arises at packing rather than at retrieval: only about four large chunks fit into the pack against about seven standard ones, and the last was cut in most packs. Chunks of 500 characters cost a few answers. Finally, multilingual-e5-large ties bge-m3 as the only channel and scores four or five answers lower inside hybrid retrieval, a difference that is not significant and lies entirely in the Persian questions. Two changes, BM25 alone and 2,000-character chunks, cost more than the detectable difference of approximately 11 answers. Under the second judge no scored change reverses direction, but the loss of 2,000-character chunks shrinks to 6 to 13 answers and e5 leads bge-m3 as the only channel by 5 to 12. % src: S16_judge2/tables.md 'Paired comparisons' J2 rows: BM25_6000 vs HYBRID_6000 -19..-14; HYBRID_C2000 vs HYBRID_6000 -13..-6, HR_C2000 vs HR_6000 -11..-8; E5_DENSE vs DENSE_6000 +5..+12 (11/3, p .0075-.33); HYDE -3..0, MQ -6..-3, HYBRID_C500 -5..-2, HR_C500 vs HR -2..-1, E5_HYBRID -7..-2

Two reference runs bound the accuracy that the reader can reach. In the closed-book run it receives no evidence and answers 26 of the 90 questions from memory, none of them company-report questions. These are called the memory-answerable questions. The gain of \lexb{} over \hybrid{} is as large when these questions are left out. In the gold-evidence run it receives only the first annotated bundle, about 570 characters, and answers 83. In four of the seven misses the quoted bundle does not tie the key value to its subject, in two a judge fails an answer on grounds that a person would probably not accept, and in one the reader leaves out a required point. The best single-pass pipelines reach 70 to 72, even at four times the budget, so most of the remaining gap lies in what the pipelines put in front of the reader.

Packing rules without a language model do not replace the call. The effect of a reranking or selection step may depend on the budget, since a larger pack leaves less to choose. Figure~\ref{fig:budget} therefore reports accuracy at five budgets from 3,000 to 24,000 characters, of which only 6,000 and 15,500 are the frozen ones.
\input{figures/budget}

The curves differ by pipeline. \hybrid{} rises steadily with the budget, whereas \hr{} gets most of its accuracy from the first 6,000 characters. On the saved answers \hr{} scores the same at 6,000 and 15,500 characters. Two later runs of the reader on the same packs put 15,500 characters five or six answers higher, so the flat curve of the saved run is reader noise. \lexb{} is flat between 6,000 and 15,500 and reaches the level of \hybrid{} at 24,000, where the differences among \hybrid{}, \lexb{} and \lexb{} without selection are negligible. Reranking and selection thus help mainly at small budgets, and \hybrid{} reaches the same accuracy when it receives several times as much text. The catch-up has a price in tokens, in line with work that weighs evidence against its cost \citep{wu2026costaware,sharify2026rerank}. \hybrid{} needs 15,500 characters to match \lexb{} at 6,000, and at that budget its reader prompt uses as many tokens as the two calls of \lexb{} together, while \hr{} reaches the same accuracy from the 6,000-character pack with less than half of those tokens. A larger budget is thus an alternative to selection, but not a free one.

The value of the selection call fades as the pack grows. At 3,000 characters, \lexb{} answers 15 to 16 more questions than \lexb{} without selection, at 6,000 characters 9 to 11 more, and at the larger budgets 2 to 4 more. The two budgets of \lexb{} used separate selector calls. When the same selector reply is packed at both budgets, the curve stays flat, so the separate calls are not the cause. The 3,000-character figure is one run with no repeated run and no second-judge score, and the budget curves rest on the first judge alone. Pooling all six readers, the budget effect is positive for \hybrid{} (odds ratio 1.77), borderline for \hr{} (1.61; $p=.076$) and near zero for \lexb{} (1.13), with no sign that it depends on the reader.

Three alternatives to a single retrieval pass were compared: an iterative search agent, the reading of whole documents, and the routing of questions to a cheaper or a more expensive pipeline. The iterative agents have the best point estimates. Each agent uses the same language model as the reader and may search the pool up to five times before it names the passages for the reader \citep{yao2023react,jin2025searchr1}. % src: R8: S3_agentic/RESULTS.md (evidence chars median 2,735 ITER_BM25, 1,652 GREP_AGENT; 15,500 allowed), S9_gpu/RESULTS.md (median 2,830; 9,070 tokens = same as ITER_BM25, 4.09x)
Each agent may hand over up to 15,500 characters, the wide frozen budget, and hands over a median of about 1,700 to 2,800. The hybrid-index agent uses about as many tokens as the BM25 agent. The BM25 agent searches with BM25 only and scores 73 of 90 under the first judge, above \hr{}, at approximately four times the tokens of \hybrid{}. Under the second judge it scores 66 to 68, level with \lexb{} (65 to 67) and with \hr{} at 15,500 characters (66 to 68). The hybrid-index agent, which searches the full hybrid index and reranks with the cross-encoder, scores 77, the highest of any variant that does not use the annotations, and 74 to 75 under the second judge. % src: S16_judge2/tables.md: HYBRID_AGENT J2 74..75; vs HR_6000 J2 +11..+12 (16/4, sign p .0118-.0266); J1 +6..+8 (15/8) Its median latency is 10.5 seconds against 3.2 seconds for \hr{}. Its lead over \hybrid{} is larger than the detectable difference, but its lead over \hr{} (6 to 8 answers) is not; under the second judge that lead is 11 to 12 (uncorrected sign $p$ from .012 to .027). The gain over the single-pass pipelines lies mostly in cross-document and English questions.

The lead of the BM25 agent depends on questions that the reader can answer without any evidence. Against \hr{}, it wins 4 of the 26 memory-answerable questions and loses 1, and on the other 64 questions the two differ by at most one answer, so it shows no measurable advantage over a cross-encoder. The hybrid-index agent keeps part of its lead. It answers 55 of the 64 questions that are not memory-answerable, which is 10 to 11 above \hybrid{} (uncorrected sign $p$ from .013 to .031) and 4 to 5 above \hr{}, a lead that is within noise. A regular-expression agent that searches the raw documents \citep{li2026directcorpus} costs approximately 5.6 times the tokens of \hybrid{} and scores 68. The comparisons of the BM25 and regular-expression agents form a family of 17 tests, and none survives Bonferroni or Holm correction. Iterative search therefore costs several times the tokens and is slower, with a gain that is not established.

Reading whole documents does not help unless the right documents are known, which is consistent with the finding that retrieval and long-context reading are complementary \citep{li2024selfroute,xu2024retrieval} and with reports that models use long contexts unevenly \citep{liu2024lost}. With the gold documents given, 30 thousand tokens of text let the reader answer 79 questions, an upper reference that no deployed system can reach. The best-matching documents by BM25, cut to the same length, give 24, because the cut text kept only a quarter of the gold passages. Routing questions by difficulty did not improve on \hr{} on the saved run, where \hr{} at 6,000 characters scores as high as at 15,500 and uses fewer tokens than any router. The repeated runs put 15,500 characters higher, so this result is tentative. An oracle that sent single-fact questions to the cheapest path would save 21\% of the tokens, whereas a router that guesses from the question text saved 4\%.

The two halves use different documents, so a difference between them mixes language and source (Sect.~\ref{sec:benchmark}). The analysis therefore reports what belongs to a language or its tooling, what appears in both halves, and what each question shows when it is asked in both languages.

Every reader model charges more tokens for a character of Persian evidence than for a character of English. For five of the six readers the ratio is between approximately 1.5 and 1.8, and for the sixth it is much higher, while English costs nearly the same under every tokenizer \citep{petrov2023tokenizers,ahia2023cost}. A character budget is therefore not a token budget for any model family tested. With the primary reader, a Persian pack of 6,000 characters costs about as many tokens as an English pack of 10,000. % src: D16: S7_analysis/RESULTS.md sec. 3 token-matched view: HYBRID +4..+7, HR +6..+8, FUSION +6..+8 at 3,000; +2..+5, -2..0, +2..+4 at 6,000 (Persian half at B, English half at the next budget)
When the English packs of \hybrid{}, \hr{} and \lexb{} without selection are enlarged to the next budget, which gives them about the token cost of the Persian packs, English accuracy rises by 4 to 8 answers at 3,000 characters and by at most 5 at 6,000.

Memorised answers differ between the halves. With no evidence at all the reader answers 20 of the 45 English questions and 6 of the 45 Persian ones, and none of the company-report questions in either language. Translating every question into the other language separates the language from the fame of the source texts. English-source questions asked in Persian are still answered from memory 18 times out of 45, whereas Persian-source questions asked in English are answered only 4 or 5 times. The memory gap therefore follows the source texts rather than the language of the question.

The same swap tests retrieval across languages \citep{asai2021xorqa,longpre2021mkqa}. The reader model translated all 90 answerable questions into the other language, keeping every name and number, and the original pool was then searched (Table~\ref{tab:language}).

BM25 collapses, because the query shares few words with the text, and the English-pool successes come mostly from Latin-script names that stayed in the Persian translations. Dense retrieval holds up, losing 3 and 5 bundles, which agrees with findings on multilingual dense retrieval and RAG \citep{chen2024bgem3,amiraz2025crosslingual,chirkova2024mrag}. To test spelling variants, the 45 Persian questions were rewritten with Arabic letters in place of the Persian ones, with the non-joiner removed or replaced by a space, and with other digit systems. The variants matter for BM25 only when the text is not normalised: with normalisation they cost at most one complete bundle among the top 40 candidates, whereas without it BM25 loses five to eight bundles and dense retrieval on raw text at most one. The text should therefore be normalised before BM25.

Finally, the search agents behave differently in the two halves. The BM25 agent gains 12 answers over \hybrid{} in English and loses 1 or 2 in Persian, and the regular-expression agent shows the same pattern. The regular-expression contrast is the only one whose difference between the halves survives the Holm correction over the 14 contrasts tested. The hybrid-index agent gains in both halves, by 3 or 4 answers in Persian and 11 in English. The cause of the gap may be either Persian itself or the Persian documents. The selection call has a positive effect in both halves at both budgets. The other differences between single-pass pipelines, such as the gain of \hr{} over \hybrid{}, lie mainly in the English half. The difference between the halves is not significant for any of them, and no within-half test is significant after Holm correction. With 45 questions a half detects a difference of only approximately 8 answers, so a result confined to one half may simply be underpowered.

Every comparison above rests on one run of the reader, one judge and, for the merged scores, one reviewer. This subsection checks how far the rankings survive a change in each of these. Two further runs of the reader on identical evidence packs show a standard deviation of a score of approximately two answers out of 90, but single scores moved by up to six: the score of \lexb{} at 6,000 characters fell by about five answers between the saved run and the two repeated runs, and that of \setr{} rose by about five. Between 9 and 17\% of the questions flip verdict across the three runs, so a difference between two pipelines measured once carries noise of approximately 2.5 answers from the reader alone. The saved run is five weeks older than the repeated runs. Every post hoc variant is compared against the saved run, so any change in the hosted reader over that time would be mixed with the effect of the variant. The selection call is not deterministic either, since it changes the evidence pack for about half of the questions when sent again.

A human review that covers the compared pipelines unequally biases the comparison. Both reviews covered questions on which the pipelines disagreed. The unequal review did not cover every pipeline on each question, and in almost half of the questions that \lexb{} won in that review, only the answer of \lexb{} had been reviewed. Because the judge is never laxer than the reviewer, each extra reviewed answer can only keep or raise the score of a pipeline, so reviewing one system more thoroughly favours it. Prediction-powered inference \citep{angelopoulos2023ppi} corrects judge scores only from a random labelled sample, which a review of disagreements is not. The unequal review gave \lexb{} a significant lead over \hybrid{} (+8, Holm-corrected $p=.043$), and the complete review, which covers all four pipelines on the same questions, gave +5 (corrected $p=.36$). The results do not identify a single best default. At 6,000 characters neither the cross-encoder nor the selection call beats \hybrid{} significantly in the primary analysis. Hybrid retrieval followed by a cross-encoder reranker is a reasonable starting point, because it needs no language-model tokens and \hr{} beat \hybrid{} in all nine pairings of repeated reader runs (mean +6 to +8). That difference is not significant, it is absent in the Persian half, and under the second judge it is 2 to 4 answers. Two points about evaluation follow. The evidence pack should be checked before the reader is blamed, because most failures are packs that hold only part of the needed evidence, and questions that the reader can answer from memory should be excluded so that they do not dilute the differences between retrieval setups.

It is important to acknowledge, however, that all guidance rests on a small test. The test has 90 answerable questions in 20 groups that share sources, so it detects only differences of approximately 11 answers, and the 95\% intervals of the two primary contrasts (Sect.~\ref{sec:frozen}) are 18 to 22 percentage points wide. Non-significant differences are not evidence of equivalence. The benchmark was drafted and source-checked by language-model agents, and only a sample was spot-checked by a person, so no independent annotator validated answerability, tiers or gold evidence, and the seven questions that the reader missed even with the gold bundle were classified by one reading of the saved records. Some English book and statute questions can be answered from memory, and Persian and English use different documents, so a result confined to one half of 45 questions may reflect a lack of power. The translations for the cross-language test were made by the reader model, and the spelling experiment measures retrieval and was not run end to end.

Most of the evidence is post hoc. The test questions had already been used before the analyses of Sects.~\ref{sec:components} to~\ref{sec:stability}, each variant was run once, and the reader and the selector are not deterministic. The six readers answer the same packs, so they are not independent replications. The human review was made by the author, who designed \lexb{}. It covers 30 questions and the primary reader only, it used a reference answer but not the full requirements, and no second reviewer took part, so neither the judge nor the reviewer is a gold standard. The pipelines are whole systems that differ in several parts, and \setr{} is an adaptation that says nothing about the published SetR. Equal evidence budgets mean equal characters in the pack and not equal cost, because the agents handed over far less than the 15,500 characters allowed, \lexb{} and \setr{} make a second call, and a character costs more tokens in Persian. The token count omits the cross-encoder, embeddings and index building. Each agent ran once, and no compression packer was tested. Graph-based and hierarchical indexing \citep{edge2024graphrag,sarthi2024raptor}, other query rewriting, late chunking and late-interaction retrieval were not tested, only two embedding models and one reranker were tried, and the chunk-size runs kept the packer settings tuned for 1,000-character chunks. The negative results are for these implementations of earlier ideas, under small pools and mostly one reader, and they do not show that the original methods fail.
