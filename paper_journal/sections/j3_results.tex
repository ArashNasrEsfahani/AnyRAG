The evaluation starts with the confirmatory test, which compares the four pipelines under the rules fixed before the test. It then examines retrieval components, traces where wrong answers come from and measures what a selection step adds. Next, the evidence budget is varied, and iterative search, long-context reading and routing are compared with single-pass pipelines. The fourth part compares Persian and English. The last part checks how far the rankings survive a repeated run of the reader, a second judge and a different review design, and it turns the findings into guidance for practitioners. Unless noted, scores are for the primary reader and the first judge on the 90 answerable questions. A net value is the number of answers by which a variant exceeds its reference. A pair such as 11/4 gives the number of questions that only the first variant answers correctly and the number that only the second does. Ranges such as 62--63 cover the answers that the judge could not score, as Sect.~\ref{sec:readers} explains.

\subsection{Confirmatory test}
\label{sec:frozen}
% src (post hoc paragraph, REVIEW2 item 16): merged scores 71, 76, 76, 78 and Holm .36 = TEST_RESULTS_HUMAN_SYM.md; setr-hybrid 7/0 p .016 and the six human_sym comparisons = paper/analysis/revision_stats.md (GLM paired comparisons); equivalence at 8: S5_stats sec. 2.2; power and 400 to 460 questions: S5_stats sec. 2.3.
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives accuracy in all eight configurations of pipeline and budget for the primary reader, and \ref{app:stats} reports every paired statistic.
\input{tables/main_glm}

The accuracy gate was not met. At the tight budget \lexb{} answered 5 to 7 more of the 90 answerable questions than \hybrid{}, but between 0 and 3 fewer than \hr{}, so it missed the required margin of 5 over \hr{}. Neither primary test was significant. The confidence intervals, which resample groups of questions that share a source, remain compatible with no improvement. The resource gate passed only under the token limit that was relaxed during development. Under the original limit it would have failed, as \ref{app:stats} shows.

The tiers show where the pipelines can differ. Single facts (T1) are near ceiling for every pipeline, so a selection step has little room to help there. On within-document questions (T2), \lexb{} and \hr{} gain over \hybrid{} while \setr{} does not. On cross-document questions (T3), \hr{} and \setr{} lead. By language, the gain of \lexb{} over \hybrid{} is visible in English and small in Persian. The development results also shrank on the test, as expected for a method that was adapted to the development set over many iterations. The lead of \lexb{} over \hybrid{} roughly halved, and its lead over \hr{} disappeared, as \ref{app:stats} shows. The confirmatory result is therefore a null result with a wide interval, and it says little about which setup to prefer.

Three post hoc analyses qualify this result. The first uses the merged scores, in which the verdicts of the author replace the judge's on the 30 reviewed questions. The merge raises every score but does not change the conclusion, because the four pipelines then answer between 71 and 78 of the 90 questions. All three pipelines with a reranking or selection step have higher point estimates than \hybrid{}, \lexb{} ties \hr{}, and the difference between \lexb{} and \hybrid{} is not significant after Holm correction over the two primary comparisons (corrected $p=.36$). \setr{} has the highest merged score, but its lead over \hybrid{} (7 wins and no loss, $p=.016$) is one of six uncorrected comparisons.

The second analysis is an equivalence test on the first-judge scores, which fails to show that \lexb{} and \hr{} are equivalent even for a margin of 8 questions out of 90. The third is a power calculation based on Eq.~\ref{eq:mde}. With 90 answerable questions and the observed disagreement rate, a paired comparison has 80\% power only for differences of approximately 11 answers. A difference of 5 answers, which was the gate, would be detected in fewer than one case in five. A difference of that relative size (5.6 percentage points) would need approximately 400 to 460 questions. The gate was therefore likely to fail whether or not selection helps, and its failure is weak evidence in either direction.

The frozen test compares whole pipelines, whereas this subsection varies single components. It first varies four parts one at a time: the retrieval channel, query rewriting, chunk size and the embedding model. Each variant changes one part of \hybrid{} or \hr{} at a budget of 6,000 characters, and Table~\ref{tab:components} lists them. The subsection then traces each wrong answer to the evidence that the reader saw and asks which step of \lexb{} matters.
\input{tables/components}

The channels differ in what they find before any reader is involved. Among the top 40 candidates, BM25 and dense retrieval find a complete evidence bundle for almost the same number of Persian questions, and dense retrieval finds more of the individual gold passages. English shows the same pattern with a somewhat larger dense advantage. Over both languages, the dense list holds a complete bundle for 71 of the 90 questions and the BM25 list for 66. BM25 also depends on text normalisation. Without Persian-aware normalisation BM25 loses two complete Persian bundles. Of the 45 Persian questions, 27 contain Persian digits, and these match the ASCII digits of the documents only after normalisation. \ref{app:posthoc} gives the recall of each channel.

Recall is not accuracy. Each channel was therefore also run alone through the same packer and reader. \hybrid{} beats both single channels at the tight budget, by 9 to 10 answers over dense retrieval and by even more over BM25, as reported for hybrid retrieval elsewhere \citep{kuzi2020hybrid,bruch2023fusion}. The gap to BM25 survives Bonferroni correction over the 12 declared comparisons, whereas the gap to dense retrieval does not and is below the detectable difference. The channels disagree on 31 of the 90 questions, and an oracle that took whichever channel was right would answer 66. Fusion helps through ordering rather than recall, since the dense list alone holds a complete bundle almost as often as the hybrid list. The pack of the dense channel does so less often, because fusion moves the chunks that both channels rank highly to the front. A larger budget removes most of the gap, as Table~\ref{tab:components} shows.

A rewritten query can reach passages that the wording of the user misses. Two common rewrites were therefore tested, and neither improved accuracy. HyDE, which retrieves with a hypothetical answer passage \citep{gao2023hyde}, and multi-query both scored below the unrewritten query, by 1 to 5 answers, and each rewrite costs one extra language-model call per question. Rewriting also lowered the number of questions for which a complete evidence bundle reached the reader, and the loss was concentrated in Persian. In English the two rewrites were a few answers better than no rewrite, which is within noise.

Chunks of 1,000 characters gave the highest accuracy. Chunks of 2,000 characters lowered it by 13 to 20 answers, depending on the pipeline. This loss arises at packing rather than at retrieval. The top 40 list held a complete bundle for more questions with large chunks than with the standard size. However, only about four large chunks fit into the pack, against about seven standard ones, and the last was cut in most packs. Chunks of 500 characters cost a few answers, and for \hybrid{} this cannot be told from no change. The losses come almost entirely from the within-document and cross-document tiers, since single-fact questions are near ceiling at every size.

Finally, the choice between the two embedding models had little effect. As the only channel, multilingual-e5-large ties bge-m3. Inside hybrid retrieval it scores four or five answers lower, a difference that is not significant and lies entirely in the Persian questions. The candidate lists are alike: with either embedding model the hybrid list holds a complete bundle for 72 of the 90 questions. Any difference therefore comes from the order of the candidates. Two changes, BM25 alone and 2,000-character chunks, cost more than the detectable difference of approximately 11 answers. No change improved on \hybrid{} with 1,000-character chunks.

Two reference runs bound the accuracy that the reader can reach. In the closed-book run it receives no evidence and answers 26 of the 90 questions from memory, and these are called the memory-answerable questions. In the gold-evidence run it receives only the first annotated bundle, about 570 characters, and answers 83, including every within-document question. Reading the saved packs, answers and verdicts of the seven misses shows that in four of them the quoted bundle does not tie the key value to its subject, in two the answer is failed by a judge on grounds that a person would probably not accept, and in one the reader leaves out a required point (Supplementary Material, Sect.~S4). The best single-pass pipelines reach 70 to 72, even at four times the budget, so most of the remaining gap lies in what the pipelines put in front of the reader. The closed-book run also tests the benchmark itself. Company-report questions are never answered from memory, whereas many English book and statute questions are. The gain of \lexb{} over \hybrid{} is as large when these 26 questions are left out.

Sixteen of the reviewed answers had been marked wrong by the judge although the pack held a complete bundle. The reviewer accepted all 16. These are therefore disagreements between judge and reviewer rather than failures of the pipeline, as Sect.~\ref{sec:stability} discusses. Cross-document questions are the ones most often left with a partial bundle, and 22 questions received a complete bundle from none of the single-pass pipelines at 6,000 characters. The iterative BM25 agent, which issues new queries, recovers them best with 71 complete bundles. \ref{app:posthoc} shows one case in which the selection call added the definition of full-time work that the fused order alone had left out.

\lexb{} without selection uses the same fused candidates and the same packer but makes no language-model call. Against the saved answers of \lexb{} it scores 9 to 11 answers lower. When the answers of \lexb{} are taken from either of the two repeated reader runs, the difference is 4 to 6 answers. The second judge gives 6 to 10, and the development questions give 8. The effect is therefore read as approximately 5 to 10 answers at 6,000 characters, and it shows in both languages. With the call, a complete bundle reaches the reader on 64 questions, against 53 without it. Without the call, \lexb{} is no better than \hybrid{}, so its fusion and packing give no measurable gain alone.

Packing rules without a language model do not replace the call. An embedding-based diversity rule (MMR) and a knapsack rule that adds whole units by score per character score well below \lexb{}. A listwise reranker, which reorders the top 20 chunks of \hybrid{} in one call, comes within three answers of \lexb{}. It stays below \hr{} under the first judge and is level with \hr{} under the second (64 against 63). A smaller selector model cuts \lexb{} to the level of \lexb{} without selection, because its replies often broke the required format and the pipeline fell back to the unselected pack. Lifting the 3-second deadline of the selector changes the score by at most a few answers.

A cross-encoder reaches the same accuracy at lower cost. Under the first judge \hr{} scores 69 to 71 against 68 to 69 for \lexb{}, and under the second judge \hr{} is 2 to 4 answers below \lexb{}, which is also within noise. \hr{} adds no language-model tokens, whereas \lexb{} needs approximately twice the tokens of \hybrid{}. Applying the selection call to the reranked candidates of \hr{} is no better than \hr{} alone, so once a cross-encoder has ordered the candidates the selection call adds no measurable gain. At 6,000 characters the pipelines therefore differ mainly in what they pack. Under the first judge the selection call has a measurable effect against the same pipeline without it (sign $p$ from .007 to .035). Under the second judge the effect is 6 to 10 answers and is not significant ($p$ from .05 to .29). The gain of \hr{} over \hybrid{} has the same sign but is not significant.

The effect of a reranking or selection step may depend on the budget, since a larger pack leaves less to choose. Figure~\ref{fig:budget} therefore reports accuracy at five budgets from 3,000 to 24,000 characters. Only the 6,000 and 15,500 points are the frozen ones.
\input{figures/budget}

The curves differ by pipeline. \hybrid{} rises steadily with the budget, whereas \hr{} gets most of its accuracy from the first 6,000 characters. On the saved answers of the primary reader, \hr{} is flat from 6,000 to 15,500 and lower at 24,000, but this is one run of the reader. Two later runs on the same packs put 15,500 characters five or six answers above 6,000. The five other readers also gain. On the saved run, \hr{} gains less from a larger pack than \hybrid{} does. The repeated runs do not confirm this, and the drop of \hr{} at 24,000 characters lies within reader noise. \lexb{} itself is flat between 6,000 and 15,500 and reaches the level of \hybrid{} at 24,000, where the differences among \hybrid{}, \lexb{} and \lexb{} without selection are negligible. Reranking and selection thus help mainly at small budgets, and \hybrid{} reaches the same accuracy when it receives several times as much text.

The catch-up has a price in tokens, in line with work that weighs evidence against its cost \citep{wu2026costaware,sharify2026rerank}. \hybrid{} needs 15,500 characters to match \lexb{} at 6,000. At that budget the reader prompt of \hybrid{} uses as many tokens as the two calls of \lexb{} together. \hr{} reaches the same accuracy from the 6,000-character pack with less than half of those tokens. A larger budget is thus an alternative to selection, but not a free one.

The two budgets of \lexb{} used separate selector calls. When one selector reply is packed at both budgets, the curve stays flat, so the separate calls do not explain it. The value of the selection call fades as the pack grows. At the smallest budget, \lexb{} answers 15 to 16 more questions than \lexb{} without selection. The gain falls to 9 to 11 at the tight budget and to between 2 and 4 at the larger budgets. The 3,000-character figure is one run with no repeated run and no second-judge score.

At 3,000 characters, \lexb{} answers 8 to 10 more questions than \hybrid{} and 3 more than \hr{}. The selected set of \setr{}, approximately 3,000 characters, answers 12 to 14 more questions than \hybrid{} at that budget and does not significantly beat \hr{}. Pooling all six readers, the budget effect is positive for \hybrid{} (odds ratio 1.77), borderline for \hr{} (1.61; $p=.076$, and $.036$ under GEE) and near zero for \lexb{} (1.13), with no sign that it depends on the reader. The budget curves rest on the first judge alone, since the second judge scored only the pipelines at 6,000 and 15,500 characters.

A character costs the primary reader approximately 1.8 times as many tokens in Persian as in English. The same character budget is therefore a larger token budget in Persian. If the two languages receive equal tokens, English packs hold approximately 1.8 times the characters, which raises accuracy most at 3,000 characters (4 to 8 answers). At 6,000 characters the gain is 2 to 5 answers for \hybrid{} and none for \hr{}, as \ref{app:posthoc} shows.

Table~\ref{tab:search} compares three alternatives to a single retrieval pass: an iterative search agent, the reading of whole documents, and the routing of questions to a cheaper or a more expensive pipeline.

The iterative agents have the best point estimates. Three agents were tested: the BM25 agent, which searches with BM25 only, the hybrid-index agent, which searches the full hybrid index and reranks with the cross-encoder, and the regular-expression agent, which searches the raw documents. Each agent uses the same language model as the reader and may search the pool up to five times before it names the passages for the reader \citep{yao2023react,jin2025searchr1,trivedi2023ircot,jiang2023flare}. The BM25 agent scores 73 of 90 under the first judge, which is above \hr{}, and it uses approximately four times the tokens of \hybrid{}. Under the second judge it scores 66 to 68. This is level with \lexb{} (65 to 67) and with \hr{} at 15,500 characters (66 to 68), and 3 to 5 above \hr{} at 6,000 characters (63). The regular-expression agent scores 67 to 68 under the second judge, so the lead of the BM25 agent over it disappears (difference $-2$ to $+1$). The hybrid-index agent scores 77 under the first judge, which is the highest of any variant that does not use the annotations, and it has no second-judge score. It uses as many tokens as the BM25 agent, and its median latency is 10.5 seconds against 3.2 seconds for \hr{}. Its lead over \hybrid{} is larger than the detectable difference, but its lead over \hr{} is not. The gain over the BM25 agent lies in Persian, and the gain over the single-pass pipelines lies mostly in cross-document and English questions. The agents also hand over little text, a median of about 1,700 to 2,700 characters, well below the 15,500 characters they were allowed.

The lead of the BM25 agent depends on questions that the reader can answer without any evidence. Table~\ref{tab:contam} repeats the main contrasts without the memory-answerable questions.
\input{tables/contam}
Against \hr{}, the BM25 agent wins 4 of these 26 questions and loses 1. On the other 64 questions the two differ by at most one answer. Without the questions that the reader answers from memory, the BM25 agent therefore shows no measurable advantage over a cross-encoder. Its search tool is also weaker than the pipelines' own, since it has neither dense retrieval nor a reranker. The hybrid-index agent keeps part of its lead, because it answers 55 of the 64 questions, which is 4 to 5 above \hr{}. It also leads \hybrid{} by 10 to 11, and the uncorrected sign test gives $p$ between .013 and .031. Table~\ref{tab:contam} lists both contrasts. The lead of the hybrid-index agent over \hr{} stays within noise, with or without the memory-answerable questions. The regular-expression agent searches the raw documents with regular expressions and reads the surrounding text, in the manner of direct corpus interaction \citep{li2026directcorpus}. It costs approximately 5.6 times the tokens of \hybrid{}, scores 68 and does no better than \hybrid{} with 15,500 characters. Persian-aware text normalisation did not improve its accuracy on Persian questions.

Reading whole documents does not help unless the right documents are known, which is consistent with the finding that retrieval and long-context reading are complementary \citep{li2024selfroute,xu2024retrieval,yu2024defenserag} and with reports that models use long contexts unevenly \citep{liu2024lost,leng2024longcontext,bai2024longbench,modarressi2025nolima}. With the gold documents given, 30 thousand tokens of text arranged around the gold passages let the reader answer 79 questions, but this is an upper reference that no deployed system can reach. Without that knowledge, the best-matching documents by BM25, cut to the same length, give 24. These documents included a gold document for 82 of the 90 questions, but the cut text kept only a quarter of the gold passages. Only the two statute pools fit the context window of the reader, and a whole-pool prompt costs approximately 210 times the tokens of a \hybrid{} question.

Finally, routing questions by difficulty cannot improve on \hr{} in this test. \hr{} at 6,000 characters already scores as high as \hr{} at 15,500, and it uses fewer tokens than any router. An oracle that sent single-fact questions to the cheapest path and the rest to \hr{} at 15,500 characters would save 21\% of the tokens of always using the wider pipeline. In contrast, a router that has to guess from the question text sent only 8 of the 30 single-fact questions to the cheap path and saved 4\%. The expected answer format, which every pipeline already receives, is a better difficulty signal, but it is derived from the annotation and is therefore not counted as a deployable router.

The comparisons of the BM25 and regular-expression agents form a family of 17 tests, and none survives Bonferroni or Holm correction for 17 tests. The comparisons of the hybrid-index agent form a separate family and are uncorrected. The conclusion is therefore limited. Iterative search costs several times the tokens and is slower. The hybrid-index agent is 14 to 15 answers above \hybrid{}, more than the detectable difference, but only 6 to 8 above \hr{}, which is not.

The two halves use different documents, so a difference between them mixes language and source, as Sect.~\ref{sec:benchmark} explains. The analysis therefore reports what belongs to a language or its tooling, what appears in both halves, and what each question shows when it is asked in both languages.

Every reader model charges more tokens for a character of Persian evidence than for a character of English, as Table~\ref{tab:tokenizers} shows.
\input{tables/language_a}
For five of the six readers the ratio is similar, between approximately 1.5 and 1.8, and for the sixth it is much higher. English costs nearly the same under every tokenizer, so the spread lies on the Persian side, in line with reports of unequal tokenizer costs across languages \citep{petrov2023tokenizers,ahia2023cost}. A character budget is therefore not a token budget for any model family tested. With the primary reader, a Persian pack of 6,000 characters costs about as much as an English pack of 10,000.

Memorised answers differ between the halves. With no evidence at all the reader answers 20 of the 45 English questions and 6 of the 45 Persian ones, and it answers none of the company-report questions in either language. This gap has two possible causes: the language of the question or the fame of the source texts. The English texts include classic books and EU law, which are better known than the Persian texts. Translating every question into the other language separates them. English-source questions asked in Persian are still answered from memory 18 times out of 45, whereas Persian-source questions asked in English are answered only 4 or 5 times. The memory gap therefore follows the source texts rather than the language of the question, and the Persian half is less affected by memorised answers.

The same swap tests retrieval across languages, as in cross-lingual question answering \citep{asai2021xorqa,longpre2021mkqa}. The reader model translated all 90 answerable questions into the other language, keeping every name and number. The original pool was then searched, and Table~\ref{tab:language} gives the result.
\input{tables/language_b}
BM25 collapses, because the query shares few words with the text. It finds a complete evidence bundle in its top 40 for few of the 45 questions of either pool, and the English-pool successes come mostly from Latin-script names that stayed in the Persian translations. Dense retrieval holds up, losing 3 and 5 bundles, which agrees with findings on multilingual dense retrieval and on multilingual RAG \citep{asai2021xorqa,chen2024bgem3,amiraz2025crosslingual,chirkova2024mrag,wu2024multilingual}. Rank fusion loses the lead that it had over dense retrieval. With the original questions, the fused pack in the Persian pool held a complete bundle for more questions than the dense pack, and with the translated questions it held fewer.

\hr{} recovers most of the loss in the Persian pool and little in the English pool. Accuracy falls as well. \hr{} answers 15 to 18 fewer of the 90 translated questions than of the original ones, and the drop is similar in both pools. For questions in a language other than that of the documents, a dense channel is therefore necessary, and rank fusion with BM25 loses its usual advantage.

Spelling variants matter for BM25 only when the text is not normalised. The 45 Persian questions were rewritten with Arabic letters in place of the Persian ones, with the non-joiner removed or replaced by a space, and with other digit systems. With normalised text, these variants cost at most one complete bundle among the top 40 candidates. The only larger loss is in the pack: removing the zero-width non-joiner costs the normalised hybrid three bundles. Without normalisation, BM25 loses five to seven bundles under Arabic letters and six to eight when all changes are combined, whereas dense retrieval on raw text loses at most one. A hybrid without normalisation keeps its recall because the dense channel compensates, but fewer complete bundles reach its pack, because the degraded BM25 ranking moves gold chunks down the fused list. The test questions were already in clean spelling, which is why the earlier ablation showed a loss of only two bundles. In practice the text should be normalised before BM25.

The search agents behave differently in the two halves. Against \hybrid{}, the regular-expression agent gains in the English half and loses in the Persian half. The BM25 agent gains 12 answers in English and loses 1 or 2 in Persian, as Table~\ref{tab:replication} shows.
\input{tables/language_c}
The regular-expression agent is the only contrast whose difference between the halves survives the Holm correction over the 14 contrasts tested. It also holds without the 26 questions that the reader can answer from memory and against both repeat runs of the hybrid control. The hybrid-index agent gains in both halves, by 3 or 4 answers in Persian and 11 in English. The gain of the BM25 and regular-expression agents in the English half did not appear in the Persian half, and the cause may be either Persian itself or the Persian documents.

The effect of the selection call has the same sign in both halves at both budgets, with no sign that the halves differ, and it is significant only in the English half at 3,000 characters. Every other single-pass effect, namely \hr{} over \hybrid{}, the larger budget and the SetR set, lies mainly in the English half. However, the difference between the halves is not significant for any of them, and no within-half test is significant after Holm correction. With 45 questions a half detects a difference of only approximately 8 answers, so a result confined to one half may simply be underpowered.

A second judge from a different model family agrees with the first on 91\% of the answers (Cohen's $\kappa=0.76$), yet it orders the middle pipelines differently at 6,000 characters. The first judge puts \hr{} on top, then \lexb{}, with \setr{} tied with \hybrid{} at the bottom. The second judge puts \lexb{} first (65 to 67), \hr{} and \setr{} next (63 and 63 to 65) and \hybrid{} last (59 to 61). Under the second judge, the gain of \lexb{} over \hybrid{} lies entirely in English questions. Both judges are stricter than the human reviewer on a similar number of the 120 reviewed answers, and neither is ever laxer. The first judge rejected 15 of the 27 \setr{} answers that the reviewer accepted, against 6 to 9 for each other pipeline. The second judge shows no such difference between pipelines. Agreement between the judges is lower on Persian answers ($\kappa=0.69$ against 0.82), but the difference is within chance (permutation $p=.21$) and one Persian question about a synonym explains much of it.

Most disagreements between the judges are not about errors. Of 66 disagreeing answers, only 7 involve a real defect. The largest group, 30 answers, have all required points right but add a true detail from the pack that the judge could not verify, because it sees only the annotated excerpts. A further 7 come from one Persian word for the Jewish minority that the first judge did not recognise. The strict rule behind these rejections is deliberate, and it explains why the judge is never laxer than a person. The second judge also scored seven post hoc variants, which Tables~\ref{tab:funnel}, \ref{tab:ablation} and~\ref{tab:search} report. Under it the BM25 agent scores 66 to 68 rather than 73, and the listwise reranker is level with \hr{}. All other post hoc variants were scored by the first judge only. These are the hybrid-index agent, the component variations, the budget sweep, selection over the reranker's candidates, the long-context and routing runs and the cross-language runs. Contrasts between such variants whose answers differ in length or style, for example the large chunks and the hybrid-index agent, could change under the second judge. The rows of Table~\ref{tab:guide} that rest on retrieval measurements, token costs or the tier structure do not depend on the judge.

A human review that covers the compared pipelines unequally biases the comparison. The unequal review covered only the questions on which the pipelines disagreed, and it did not cover the pipelines equally. In almost half of the questions that \lexb{} won in that review, only the answer of \lexb{} had been reviewed. Because the judge is never laxer than the reviewer, each extra reviewed answer can only keep or raise the score of a pipeline, so reviewing one system more thoroughly favours it. Prediction-powered inference corrects judge scores from a labelled sample \citep{angelopoulos2023ppi}, but only when that sample is random, which a review of disagreements is not. The unequal review gave \lexb{} a significant lead over \hybrid{} (+8, Holm-corrected $p=.043$ over the two primary comparisons). In the complete review, which covers all four pipelines on the same questions, the lead was +5 (corrected $p=.36$). Every compared system should therefore be reviewed on each selected question, and disagreement should be reported per system. More generally, a ranking from one run, one judge and one partial review should be read as a tie unless the difference is large.

The results do not identify a single best default. At 3,000 characters the selection call has a clear effect against \lexb{} without selection, but at 6,000 characters neither the cross-encoder nor the selection call beats \hybrid{} significantly in the primary analysis. Hybrid retrieval followed by a cross-encoder reranker is a reasonable starting point for two reasons. It needs no language-model tokens, and \hr{} beat \hybrid{} in all nine pairings of repeated reader runs (mean +6 to +8). That difference is not significant, it is absent in the Persian half, and under the second judge it is 2 to 4 answers. Every language-model alternative that was tried, whether listwise reranking, set selection or the iterative agent, cost three to four times the tokens without a clear gain. The component experiments add that both retrieval channels should be kept, chunks cut at about 1,000 characters and query rewriting skipped.

Several steps help only in narrow conditions. A selection call helps when it is the only reranking or selection step, so it is an option when no GPU is available for a reranker, but it gives no measurable gain on top of a cross-encoder. Showing more text works in the same case, since \hybrid{} catches up to a selector at about two and a half times the characters. Iterative search over the full hybrid index has the highest point estimate, from one run and one judge, but it needs four times the tokens and three times the median latency. For Persian, the best-supported recommendations rest on direct measurements and not on a contrast between the two halves. Budgets should be set in tokens, spelling and digits should be normalised before BM25, and a dense channel is necessary when the question language differs from the document language. Two points about evaluation follow. First, the evidence pack should be checked before the reader is blamed, because most failures are packs that hold only part of the needed evidence. Second, questions that the reader can answer from memory should be excluded, so that they do not dilute the differences between retrieval setups.

It is important to acknowledge, however, that all guidance rests on a small test. The test has 90 answerable questions in 20 groups that share sources, so it detects only differences of approximately 11 answers. The intervals of the main contrasts are 18 to 22 percentage points wide, and non-significant differences are not evidence of equivalence. The benchmark was drafted and source-checked by language-model agents, and only a sample was spot-checked by a person. No independent annotator validated answerability, tiers or gold evidence, and the seven questions that the reader missed even with the gold bundle were classified by one reading of the saved records (Supplementary Material, Sect.~S4). Some English book and statute questions can be answered from memory, and Persian and English use different documents. Each half has only 45 questions and detects a difference of approximately 8 answers, so an effect confined to one half may reflect a lack of power. The translations for the cross-language test were made by the reader model, many English-pool translations keep Latin-script names that help BM25, and the spelling experiment measures retrieval and was not run end to end.

Most of the evidence is post hoc. The test questions had already been used before the analyses of Sects.~\ref{sec:components} to~\ref{sec:stability}. Each variant was run once, and the reader and the selector are not deterministic. The six readers answer the same packs, so they are not independent replications, and the post hoc variants were scored by the first judge only, apart from the seven that the second judge also scored. The human review was made by the author, who designed \lexb{}. It covers 30 questions and the primary reader only, and it used a reference answer but not the full requirements. No second reviewer took part. Neither the judge nor the reviewer is therefore a gold standard. The pipelines are whole systems that differ in several parts, and the ablations isolate only some of them. \setr{} is an adaptation and says nothing about the published SetR.

Finally, several scope limits apply. Equal evidence budgets mean equal characters in the pack. They do not mean equal cost, because the agents were allowed up to 15,500 characters but handed over far less, \lexb{} and \setr{} make a second call, and a character costs more tokens in Persian. The token count covers query-time language-model calls and omits the cross-encoder, embeddings and index building, and latency was measured while other jobs ran on the same machine and is descriptive. Each agent ran once, the BM25 and regular-expression agents have no dense channel, and no compression packer was tested. Graph-based RAG such as GraphRAG \citep{edge2024graphrag}, hierarchical indexing such as RAPTOR \citep{sarthi2024raptor}, other query rewriting \citep{wang2023query2doc,ma2023queryrewriting,zheng2024stepback}, proposition-level or late chunking \citep{chen2024densex,gunther2024latechunking} and late-interaction retrieval \citep{khattab2020colbert,santhanam2022colbertv2} were not tested end to end, so the guidance says nothing about them. The component comparisons change one choice at a time around one pipeline and one primary reader, so interactions between components are untested. Only two embedding models and one reranker were tried. The chunk-size runs kept the packer settings that were tuned for 1,000-character chunks. The negative results are for these implementations of earlier ideas, under small pools and mostly one reader, and they do not show that the original methods fail.
